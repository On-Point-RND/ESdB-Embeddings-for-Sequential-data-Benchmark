Multi-domain sequence representation learning

Towards a Universal Sequence Embedding

A Multi-Domain, Multi-Task Benchmark for Self-Supervised Methods

Can one sequence embedding serve many downstream tasks? Could self-supervised methods from different domains transfer to event sequences?
We provide a comprehensive study of these questions through a new unified benchmark we introduce, comprising 12 datasets covering transactions, recommender logs, time series, and text. Seven self-supervised methods from different domains are evaluated across classification, regression, forecasting, and anomaly detection under a single protocol.

Core findings

  1. SSL approaches are not universal.
  2. There is a large discrepancy between classification and regression or forecasting performance.
  3. Current SSL approaches do not adequately account for temporal structure.
  4. Current backbones may not be well suited to modelling temporal components.
  5. Architectural design determines which tasks a model performs well on—for example, through feature aggregation.
  6. Validation models are biased toward specific tasks; consequently, HPO is biased as well.
  7. Models designed for one task transfer poorly to others.
  8. We therefore need new models and evaluation procedures—or we can combine embeddings from diverse existing encoders.

We invite researchers to validate their new models on our benchmark.

Overview

Success on one downstream task transfers only weakly to another. Training loss and label-free quality metrics do not reliably identify a universal model, while per-task selection introduces validator bias. Fusion offers a practical alternative.

Cross-task transferability

Task performance and method rankings are only weakly aligned, even within the same dataset. A representation selected for one task therefore cannot be assumed to work equally well for others.

Aggregation depends on the target configuration.

The last embedding is strongly preferred for forecasting and moderately preferred for regression, while mean pooling is more common for classification and anomaly detection. The same overall pattern holds across methods, with some differences in strength.

Validators introduce measurable bias during hyperparameter optimization.

LightGBM has the best average candidate rank, but MLP favours forecasting while the linear probe favours anomaly detection. The same frozen embeddings can be ordered differently by the readout.

23 settings · six datasets · final 10 Optuna trials

Label-free signals are noisy and do not reliably predict downstream performance.

SSL loss is not a reliable selection signal.

Across datasets and models, SSL loss provides no stable signal for either Optuna-based model selection or early stopping: its relationship with downstream performance varies widely across tasks and settings.

Unsupervised metrics do not reliably identify the best checkpoint.

We evaluate RankMe, LiDAR, and aSMI as potential label-free signals across downstream tasks, but their behavior remains noisy. Different variants peak at different checkpoints and do not consistently recover the downstream-best model.

A random encoder is a Strong Baseline.

On some datasets, trained SSL methods add only a small increment above R-RNN or R-Tr; on Favorita regression they all underperform the random RNN. The frontier view separates the contribution of architecture from the contribution of pretraining.

Grey represents performance of random projections.

Hyperparameter optimisation is necessary.

Fixed-budget Optuna studies improve downstream metrics substantially—up to 20% in the main analysis, with larger absolute changes in hard settings. Plateau trials vary strongly by dataset, motivating the 70-trial NTP budget.

Multi-target HPO reduces search cost without a consistent loss in quality.

Instead of running a separate HPO study for each downstream task, we optimize all tasks jointly within a single study. This requires 4× fewer trials on X5-Retail and 3× fewer on Twitter, while neither single- nor multi-target HPO consistently wins in downstream performance.

Evaluation protocol

01

Global Time Split

A shared test boundary is the 0.9 quantile of valid target times. Earlier histories train the encoder and validator; future shifts test them.

02

Global User Split

Complete entity histories are assigned approximately 90/10 to train and test, so downstream evaluation uses unseen entities.

03

Shift construction

Each local shift stores a history prefix plus a horizon-specific future target. Up to 9/1 or 90/10 train/test shifts are sampled per entity.

04

Frozen evaluation

A linear probe is trained with three random seeds. MLP and LightGBM are added on six datasets to quantify validator sensitivity.

Reproducible by construction.

Processed datasets share a Parquet schema, frequency-ranked categorical values, robustly scaled numerical features, saved split metadata and seed-controlled shift sampling. Exact dataset counts and target horizons are available through the cards above.
